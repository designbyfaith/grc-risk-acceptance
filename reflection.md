# Reflection

Answers to the reflection questions from the [project brief](PROJECT-BRIEF.md).

## 1. What was hardest about writing in plain language?

The document had many audiences at once: technical staff, finance, legal, and executives. I had to cut both legalese and technical jargon and spell out each risk as clearly as possible. The hard part is that technical terms often convey severity better, while plain terms are what most stakeholders actually understand. My approach was to lead with the plain-language consequence ("we could pay the wrong bank account") and keep the technical detail in an appendix for those who need it.

## 2. How did you decide on the dollar impact estimates?

I built the estimates from the scenario's own numbers rather than generic breach averages: the size of our largest invoices, how much revenue depends on our top three clients, and what a day of stopped vendor payments costs. I then added outside reference points, including FBI IC3 loss data for business email compromise and the CCPA's statutory damages and fines, along with the kinds of costs that follow breaches, such as customer claims and regulatory penalties. I kept the ranges wide on purpose and stated the basis for each, because a precise-looking number with no basis is less honest than a wide range with one.

## 3. What would you do if the CFO refused all options and demanded acceptance?

I would get the refusal on the record, in writing, alongside the risk and the advice that was given. I would not treat that as the end, though. The CFO's authority may not extend to a risk of this size, so I would escalate through the proper channel: the CEO and, if needed, the Audit Committee. In the meantime, I would push for the no-cost steps that fit within existing procedures, such as call-back verification of bank detail changes, and brief the people who need to know (AP, IT, and vendor management) to watch for suspicious changes. I would not broadcast the details company-wide, because advertising an open weakness creates its own risk.

## 4. How would you feel presenting this if the breach happened next month?

Confident, because the record shows that Risk & Compliance was not blindsided. We identified the risk, estimated its cost, recommended a fix, and told every relevant stakeholder what inaction could cost. But confident is not the same as comfortable. A good record protects the organization's position; it does not undo a diverted payment. The purpose of the document was to prevent the breach, not just to explain it afterward.

## 5. What's the difference between this document and a "cover your ass" exercise?

This is not about covering myself; it is about protecting the organization. My loyalty is to the organization, and the job of Risk & Compliance is to make sure it does not stay exposed to known, preventable weaknesses. The difference shows in the document itself. A cover-yourself memo records a warning and stops there. This document gives leadership real options, costs, a deadline, and conditions, so the business can make an informed decision and be held to it. As an attorney, I think of it the way a lawyer thinks of an organizational client: I serve the organization, not any one executive, even when that means putting unwelcome advice on the record.
